# Cold Lead DM — the approved warm-visitor email, massaged for cold

This is the framework the cold campaign generator writes against. It is
`15_warm_visitor_hybrid.md` with the beats that assume a website visit removed, and nothing
else changed. Read that file first; this one records only the deltas.

## Why it is a derivative and not a new script

The warm hybrid was approved because of its middle: a personalized pain hook built from the
prospect's actual stack, a one-line solution, and the sample-dashboard CTA. None of that
depends on the visit.

So the structure is preserved and only the visit-dependent parts change. Rewriting more than
that would throw away the thing that got approved.

## What changes, and what does not

| Beat | Warm visitor | Cold lead |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Open | "Thanks for stopping by the site." | **A flat, specific observation about them.** No visit to thank. |
| 2. Pain hook | Their stack → the consequence | **Unchanged.** This is the approved core. |
| 3. Solution, one line | Unchanged | **Unchanged.** |
| 4. CTA | Sample dashboard on their stack | **Unchanged. And it is now the last line.** |
| 5. Open door | "Tell me what brought you to the site." | **Deleted.** See below. |

## Beat 1 — the cold open

A cold lead has done nothing to acknowledge, so there is nothing warm to say and no reason
to pretend otherwise. **Do not manufacture a relationship.** The open is a flat statement of
something true and specific about them, exactly as the Audience-Building DM does it:

> "Hi Matthew, you got 11,831 followers on LinkedIn."

State the observation. Do not explain how you know it, do not apologize for the email, and
do not warm it up. Specificity is what earns the second line; nothing else does.

**The method is never mentioned.** No "I noticed", no "I was looking at your site", no "I
came across your company". These are the cold-email tells that make a good observation read
like surveillance. We detect public signals; the message should be comfortable being read
that way, which means stating the fact and moving on.

Because the open is now an observation about their stack, beats 1 and 2 sit closer together
than in the warm version. Let them run into each other. Do not pad them apart.

## Beat 5 is deleted — one call to action, never two

The warm version closed with a soft open door after the CTA: *"or tell me what brought you
to the site."* On a warm lead that is a second, lower-friction way to reply.

**On a cold lead it is a second call to action, and a cold message never gets two.** Two
asks split the reply between them and make neither one the obvious thing to do. The reader
has to choose, and the most common choice is to do nothing.

So the email ends on the CTA. After it comes the sign-off and nothing else. In particular:

- No "or tell me how you handle it today".
- No "or let me know if this is relevant".
- No second question of any kind after the ask, in its own paragraph or tacked onto the end
  of the CTA sentence.

The CTA is already the low-friction ask. It offers a gift rather than a meeting, it proves
the claim instead of asserting it, and it is answerable with one word. It does not need a
softer companion, and giving it one weakens it.

## Never state a count of their systems

The pain hook names their tools. It must **never** say how many they have.

> ✗ "Ironbark Consulting operates nine distinct reporting systems."
> ✗ "That's three different answers to one question."
> ✓ "Salesforce, Marketo and Google Analytics 4 each hold a piece of it, and none of them
>    holds the whole thing."

Two reasons, and the second is the important one:

1. **It reads as intrusive.** A precise count of someone's internal systems sounds like it
   came from somewhere it shouldn't have.
2. **It is an easy way to be wrong.** The detected stack is what is publicly visible, never
   the whole of it. They almost certainly run tools we cannot see, so any specific number is
   probably false, and being confidently wrong in line two ends the email.

Name the tools, describe the consequence, and let the quantity stay vague. "Each hold a
piece", "no single place", "depends which one you open" all carry the same meaning without
asserting a number. Educated guesses about their situation are fine; counts are not.

## Never use an em dash

No em dashes (`—`) or en dashes (`–`) anywhere in the copy, in the subject or the body.
Use a comma, a full stop, or start a new sentence. This applies to every variant.

## Everything else is the warm framework

Length **40–75 words**, shorter than the warm version because a beat is gone. Short
sentences, a blank line between beats, plain sentence case. Name two or three of their
actual tools, never one they don't run. Say what having them separately costs. No feature
lists, no price, no discount. Sign off with a first name only.

## What kills a cold one specifically

- **A second ask.** See above. This is the one that costs replies.
- **A number attached to their stack.** See above.
- **Warm language.** Any thanks for a visit, any reference to "your interest", any implied
  prior contact. It is provably false and it is the fastest way to lose the reply.
- **Announcing the email.** "I'm reaching out because", "quick question", "hope you don't
  mind the cold email". The message should start at the observation.
- **Explaining how you found them.** See beat 1.
- **A generic first line.** Warm emails could survive a soft open because the visit did the
  personalizing. Cold cannot. If line one would send to any company unchanged, the email is
  dead and no later line rescues it.
